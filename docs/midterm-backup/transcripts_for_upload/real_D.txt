Counselor: Hi D, thank you for staying a little after work to do this. Is it okay if I record our conversation?

Counselor: You can skip any question you don't want to answer. And please don't tell me anything about immigration papers or document numbers.
Student: Okay. Thank you.
Counselor: How long have you been in the U.S., and what does a normal week look like for you?
Student: Two years. Two years in March. My week... Monday to Friday I clean houses. Two houses every day, sometimes three. In the morning I take Valentina to school, and my sister pick her up in the afternoon. Saturday I clean only one house, half day. Sunday is for church and for Valentina.
Counselor: What has been the hardest part of living here so far?
Student: The hardest... the language. Is very difficult for me. I understand more than I can speak. When people talk fast, I am lost. And the rent. Everything is very expensive here.
Counselor: What do you miss most about home?
Student: My mom. She is in Cali. And the food. My mom make sancocho on Sundays, and all the family comes. Here is only me, my sister, her husband and the kids. Is nice, but is not the same.
Counselor: When something goes wrong, who do you talk to?
Student: My sister. She is here eight years, she know more. And I call my mom, but I don't tell her the bad things. She worry too much.
Counselor: Can you tell me about a time you felt out of place, or unsure you belonged here?
Student: Hmm. At Valentina's school. The meeting with the teacher. The other moms talk with the teacher, they ask many questions. I just say "yes, yes, thank you." Valentina translate for me. She is six. I feel a little bad. The mom should help the daughter, not the daughter help the mom.
Counselor: Do you feel pressure to make this move worth it for anyone?
Student: Yes. For Valentina. I come here for her. For her future, the school, everything. So I have to work hard. And for my mom. I send money every month for her medicine.
Counselor: Is there anything that keeps you up at night?
Student: Sometimes the money. The rent go up again in January. And when Valentina is sick, I worry, because if I don't work, I don't get paid.
Counselor: How is your energy now compared to when you first arrived?
Student: More tired, I think. The first months I was excited, everything is new. Now my back hurts sometimes, from the cleaning. But is okay. I am strong.
Counselor: What has helped you get through the harder days?
Student: God, and the church. And Valentina. She sing the songs from school in English, and she correct my English. "Mami, is not 'she have,' is 'she has.'"
Counselor: You said it's okay. What does okay look like on a normal day?
Student: Okay is... I work, I come home, I cook, I sleep. No problems. Some days I am very tired and a little sad, but the next day is better.
Counselor: What did you have to figure out here that works differently than in Colombia?
Student: Many things. The bus. The bank, I needed help to open the account. The doctor. Here you need appointment for everything, and you wait many weeks. In Colombia you go to the clinic and they see you.
Counselor: When you need something like a doctor, a bank account or housing, how do you figure out where to start?
Student: I ask my sister. Or the lady at church, she help many people. If not, I ask Valentina to look in the phone.
Counselor: How do conversations in English feel at work, compared to with friends?
Student: At work is okay, because the work is always the same. The clients say "please clean the kitchen, the bathroom," I understand. But a long conversation, no. With my friends I speak Spanish, so no problem.

Student: The school send many papers and emails. Field trip, picture day, money for something. Sometimes I don't understand and I miss things. One time everybody wear pajamas to school, and Valentina no. She was sad. I feel very bad that day.

Student: No. In Colombia I was auxiliar de enfermería, like a nurse assistant, for five years in a hospital. Here they say I need to study again, and take the English test. So I clean houses. Is honest work. I am not ashamed.
