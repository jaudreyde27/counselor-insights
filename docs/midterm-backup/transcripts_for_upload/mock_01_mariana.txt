Counselor: Thanks for meeting with me, Mariana. Before we start, is it still okay that I record this?
Student: Yes, yes, no problem.
Counselor: And you can skip anything. Also, please don't share details about your immigration case or any document numbers.
Student: Okay. That's easy, I don't understand my case anyway.
Counselor: How long have you been here now, and what does a normal week look like?
Student: Eight months. Eight months and... one week? A normal week, okay. I take Mateo to school, then I sleep a little, then at five I go to work, I clean offices downtown, until one, one-thirty in the morning. Saturday also. Sunday is for Mateo. We go to the park, or to Walmart. Very exciting life.
Counselor: What has been the hardest part of living here so far?
Student: Hmm. The hardest... Honestly? The driving. Everything is so far in Houston. Without a car you are nobody. No, I'm joking. Well, it's true, but... I don't know. It's a lot of things. Small things.
Counselor: Can you tell me about one of the small things?
Student: Like... the school sends papers, forms, and I read them with the translator on the phone, and I'm not sure I understand right. Last month I missed picture day. Mateo was the only one in normal clothes. He didn't care. I cared. It's silly.
Counselor: It doesn't sound silly.
Student: Mm.
Counselor: You mentioned work. How is work going?
Student: Work is fine. Honestly, I'm lucky to have it. My friend from church helped me get it. The supervisor is nice.
Counselor: What kind of work did you do in Venezuela?
Student: I was a dentist. Pediatric dentist, for kids. Nine years. I had my own... well, with a partner, a small clinic. But that's, you know. That's over there.
Counselor: What's it like going from that to cleaning offices?
Student: Well, now I clean the bathrooms of the lawyers instead of looking in the mouths of the children. Es que... I don't know how to answer that. It's work. Work is work. Mateo eats.
Counselor: That's fair. Is there anything you miss about that work?
Student: The kids. The kids who were afraid, and I make them laugh, and at the end they want the sticker. Sí. I miss being... somebody who knows what she is doing. Here I am always asking, asking. "Sorry, can you repeat." "Sorry, what is this form." Always sorry.
Counselor: Always sorry.
Student: Yes. The Venezuelans here say I say sorry more than the Americans.
Counselor: Is getting back into dentistry something you've looked into?
Student: I looked one time. You have to do the exams again, two years of school again, it's like eighty thousand dollars, I don't know exactly. I closed the computer. Maybe when Mateo is in university.
Counselor: When something goes wrong, who do you talk to?
Student: My sister, she's in Madrid. We talk on WhatsApp. But the time is different, when I finish work she is waking up. And my mom, but I don't tell my mom the problems. She has her own problems.
Counselor: And here in Houston?
Student: Here, my neighbor Gladys. She watches Mateo at night. She is from Honduras, very sweet. But she has three kids also, so... I don't like to ask too much. Last week Mateo had a fever and I had to call in sick, and the supervisor was okay, but they didn't pay me. So.
Counselor: How did that week go, money-wise?
Student: We managed.
Counselor: Is there anything that keeps you up at night?
Student: I work at night, so... No, I understand the question. Sometimes, yes. The rent went up. The school says Mateo needs glasses. I have the card from the clinic but I don't know what it covers. I keep the papers in a folder and I look at them and... I close the folder. I'm very good at closing things.
Counselor: Who would you ask about the insurance?
Student: I don't know. The clinic? I called once, I waited forty minutes, then I had to go to work.
Counselor: What has helped you get through the harder days?
Student: Mateo. He learns English so fast, he corrects me now. "Mami, it's not 'the chicken is very rich,' it's 'delicious.'" And the church. On Sunday I feel normal for two hours.
Counselor: Have you been treated differently because of where you're from or how you speak?
Student: Hmm. Not really bad. Sometimes people talk to me slow, very loud, like I am stupid. One time a man in an office I clean left a note, "please clean better," with a drawing. Like I can't read. I can read. I read in two languages. Anyway.
Counselor: That sounds frustrating.
Student: It's okay. He's just one man.
Counselor: Is there anything we didn't ask about that matters to you?
Student: No... I think, no. Well. Maybe. If you know about programs for, like, foreign professionals, I would like to know. Not now. Just for the future.
Counselor: I'll look into that. Last question: how comfortable were you being recorded? Was there anything you held back?
Student: It was okay. At first strange. I didn't talk about my case, because you said, and also I don't like to talk about it. And how I left Venezuela, that's... another day. Another coffee.
