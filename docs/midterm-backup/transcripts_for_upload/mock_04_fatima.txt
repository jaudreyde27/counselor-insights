Counselor: Hello, Fatima. Thank you for coming. Is it okay to record?
Student: Yes. Okay.
Counselor: You can say "skip" for any question. And please don't tell me about immigration papers or numbers.
Student: Okay. Skip. I know this word.
Counselor: How long have you been in Sacramento?
Student: Ten month. Almost eleven.
Counselor: What does a normal week look like?
Student: Monday to Thursday, morning, I go to English class. At the adult school. Afternoon, I cook, I clean, I study. My husband, he works in the... how to say... the place for cars. He fix cars? No, he wash cars, and now he learn to fix. He work very long. Come home eight, nine.
Counselor: What has been the hardest part of living here?
Student: Hardest... Language. Everything is language. Doctor, language. Store, language. Bus, language. I am like a child here. I don't like this.
Counselor: Like a child how?
Student: In Kabul I was student of law. Third year. I read big books. Here I cannot read the letter from the gas company. My teacher say I learn fast. But not fast for me.
Counselor: Do you want to go back to studying law?
Student: Yes. Inshallah. My teacher say first community college, then university. Maybe five year, six year. I will be old. Thirty-three. It's okay.
Counselor: What do you miss most about home?
Student: My mother. My sister. Our house has a small garden, my mother grows... um, I don't know the word. Gul. Flowers. Roses. In the morning we drink tea there.
Counselor: Are your mother and sister still there?
Student: My mother is in Pakistan now, with my uncle. My sister is... still there. I don't want to talk about my sister. Sorry.
Counselor: That's okay. We don't have to.
Student: Thank you.
Counselor: When something goes wrong, who do you talk to?
Student: My husband. But he is tired. When he come home, he eat and sleep. I don't want to give him more problem. My mother, on the phone, but the internet is bad there.
Counselor: Anyone here in Sacramento?
Student: In English class there is a woman, Yasmin, from Syria. We talk... half English, half hands. She is my friend. Other people from Afghanistan, there are many here, but I don't know them so well.
Counselor: During the day, when you're home, how is that?
Student: Quiet. Very quiet. In Kabul my house always has people. Aunt, cousin, neighbor. Here I sit, I watch the TV, I don't understand the TV. It's okay. I study.
Counselor: Is there anything that keeps you up at night?
Student: Sometime. I check the phone. The news. Can we skip?
Counselor: Of course.
Counselor: When you need something like a doctor, how do you figure out where to start?
Student: The caseworker from the agency, she help us at first. Now we finish the program, so... I don't know. Last month I have pain here, I don't know where to go. I wait. Now it's better.
Counselor: If it came back, what would you do?
Student: I don't know. Maybe I ask Yasmin.
Counselor: Is there anything about money here you had to learn?
Student: My husband does the money. I don't know about this.
Counselor: Has anything made you hesitant to ask for help?
Student: Hm. I don't know how to answer this. My problems, they are small. Other people, they lost everything. I have husband, I have apartment, I have class. I should be thankful.
Counselor: You can be thankful and still have hard things.
Student: Yes. Maybe.
Counselor: What has helped you on the harder days?
Student: English class. When I learn a new word, I feel... alive? Is it the right word? And I pray. And Yasmin.
Counselor: Is there anything we didn't ask that matters to you?
Student: I want to know how to start the community college. The papers.
Counselor: How comfortable were you being recorded?
Student: It's okay. A little scared at start. My English is not good, I don't want people to hear.
