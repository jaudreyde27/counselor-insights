Student: The talking. In section, the TA asks, "What do people think?" And everyone thinks out loud. Americans can think out loud. I can only think inside, then translate, then check, then maybe speak. By then they're on the next slide.

Student: Sometimes. Once per section, like a quota. I prepare one sentence and say it in the first ten minutes, so I don't have to worry anymore. After that I'm free.
Counselor: That's a strategy.
Student: It's a survival strategy. At Fudan, I was the one people asked. I answered in class, I helped everyone before exams. Here my brain is the same brain, but it comes out of my mouth at fifty percent.
Counselor: Fifty percent?
Student: Maybe forty, if I'm tired.
Counselor: When you first got here, what did you want your life to look like?
Student: Hmm. I had a very... big idea. I thought, okay, this is maybe the only time in my life I can really go outside China. Not as a tourist. Living. If I only stay with Chinese friends, then why did I come? I could have done a master's in Shanghai and had the same life with worse weather.
Counselor: So you wanted non-Chinese friends.
Student: Yes. I really tried. I went to the international welcome events, the department mixer, the, what is it, the ice cream social.
Counselor: How did those go?
Student: At the ice cream social I ate three ice creams, because if you're holding ice cream, you have something to do with your hands. People came over and said, "Hi! Where are you from? They asked me two questions, I asked them two questions back, then it was quiet, and they said "Nice to meet you!" and left. Every conversation died at question four.
Counselor: Question four.
Student: There's a wall at question four. I can do one, two, three. Four needs real English. Or real... I don't know. Something I don't have.
Counselor: Was there a time you really put yourself out there?
Student: The band. Okay, you know this story a little.
Counselor: Tell it for the recording.
Student: Okay. Early in the quarter there was recruiting for one of the student bands at Stanford. I played keyboard in a band at Fudan. A very bad band, we only played at parties. But I thought, this is music, music doesn't need English. So I went.
Counselor: How was it?
Student: The playing part was fine. They let me play a little, people clapped, someone said "Nice!" After, people were standing around, and I did the brave thing. I asked a few people, "Can I add your WhatsApp?" Four people. I counted. Four people.
Counselor: That's a lot of courage.
Student: For me it was like a whole year of courage, in one evening. I went home so happy. I sent each of them a message: "Hi, it was so nice to meet you today! Hope we can play together!" I checked the grammar on my phone twice.
Counselor: And?
Student: One person replied the next day. "Yeah for sure!" Then nothing. One replied three days later with a thumbs up. Two never replied. And when I saw them on campus later, they were nice, they said "hey!", but it was like... I'm not really there for them. Like they don't really care.
Counselor: How did that feel?
Student: Sad. I don't want to be dramatic. It's not a big thing. But I was so proud of that courage, and then... you know when you blow up a balloon really big, and someone pokes it? Chuōpò le (popped, punctured). My courage got popped.
Counselor: Did you hear back from the band?
Student: They picked someone else. Which is fine. Their other keyboard player was really good. But honestly, I wanted a friend more than a spot.
Counselor: Why do you think they didn't reply?
Student: I really don't understand. That's the part I'm most confused about. Why do Americans reply to messages so slowly? In China, on WeChat, if someone messages you, you reply in ten minutes. If you wait three days, it means you hate them. Here, three days is... normal? Is it normal? My classmate said, "Oh, I'm just bad at texting." Everyone here says they're bad at texting. How is everyone bad at texting? It's a whole country of people who are bad at texting. I'm not angry. I'm just... confused. Very confused. I need a manual.
Counselor: Are there other unwritten rules like that?
Student: So many. Okay, the car one. You remember the car one.
Counselor: I remember.
Student: For the recording. In China, if someone is driving, the front seat is kind of... for their boyfriend, girlfriend, husband, wife. Their person. A friend doesn't automatically sit there. Sitting in the back is normal, even polite.
Counselor: And here...
Student: Here, if someone picks you up and you sit in the back, it's like you're treating them as an Uber driver. I did this. Twice, before you told me. The first time, a classmate drove a few of us to Costco. I opened the back door and got in, and she just... paused. Then she said, "Okay! Where to, ma'am?" Everyone laughed. I laughed too. I didn't understand why it was funny until you explained it, like a week later. Then I wanted to die. Retroactively.
Counselor: Retroactively.
Student: I replayed it in my head in bed. So embarrassing.
Counselor: What else?
Student: Small talk. A lot of the time I just don't understand it. Not the words, I know the words. But what they're doing. Someone says "How's it going?" and I start to answer, and they're already gone. Or a group is talking, and suddenly everyone laughs, and I laugh too, but I don't know what we're laughing at. I'm just laughing with the room. Like a laugh track.
Counselor: Does that happen a lot?
Student: Every day. I'm a professional laugh track.
Counselor: Anything else?
Student: Gifts! This one was recent. A girl in my program had a birthday, so I brought her a gift. Chinese tea, a nice one from Guangzhou, in a box. And she opened it. Right there, in front of everyone. She read the box out loud: "Oh my god, what is this? Is this tea?" Everyone looked. And I was standing there like, please, put it down, open it at home.
Counselor: Because in China...
Student: In China you take the gift home and open it later. Opening it in front of the person is a little strange. You say thank you, you put it away. So when she opened it, I was so nervous. What if she doesn't like it? Now I have to watch her face not liking it. She liked it, I think. She said, "I love this!" But Americans say "I love this" about everything. I can't tell.
Counselor: That's fair.
Student: There's always a hidden rule, and everyone knows it except me. It's like everyone got the rulebook, and mine was in another language.
Counselor: How does life here compare to Fudan?
Student: Honestly? I'm happier here. That surprised me. I didn't expect to say that.
Counselor: Why happier?
Student: At Fudan, everyone studied so hard. Medicine at Fudan is... you know. Everyone was the top student in their whole province. Everyone studies until one a.m. The only question is your rank. Nobody asks, "Why are you doing this?" You just do it, because the next exam is coming.
Counselor: And here?
Student: Here people care about... meaning. Meaning in life. One classmate is doing epi because her hometown had a water contamination problem. Someone left a finance job because he "wanted to do something meaningful." People ask me, "What are you passionate about?" At first I thought it was a strange question. Passionate? I'm passionate about passing. But now I think about it. It's a good question.
Counselor: So that part is better.
Student: That part is much better. I sleep more. I see the sun. Palo Alto has too much sun, actually.
Counselor: And socially?
Student: Socially it's the opposite. At Fudan I had so many friends. My roommates, my class, the band. I was smart, funny, people wanted to sit with me. Here, outside the Chinese circle, I don't know how to make friends. I really don't know how. It's not that I don't try. I don't know the steps.
Counselor: You're really funny, though.
Student: In Chinese! In Chinese I'm very funny. My jokes are all internet words. If I'm annoyed, I say wǒ zhēn de huì xiè (literally "I really will thank you," a sarcastic "are you serious?"). Or juéjuézi (an over-the-top "absolutely amazing"). Or I just send a sticker of a cat lying flat on the floor. Everyone gets it.
Counselor: And in English?
Student: In English you can't translate it. I tried once. I said "I will thank you" sarcastically to a classmate, and she said, "Oh! You're welcome!" So sincere. The joke died. I had to watch it die. And I don't know what's popular here, what people are saying now. Like this "six seven" thing. People say "six seven!" and everyone laughs. My whole section laughed for one minute. I asked the girl next to me, "What does it mean?" She said, "Nothing! That's the point!" Then why are we laughing? I studied medicine for five years. I can't understand two numbers.
Counselor: Nobody understands six seven.
Student: But they all laugh together! That's the thing. It's not about understanding, it's about being inside. And I'm outside, looking in.
Counselor: Do you ever explain your jokes?
Student: No. If you explain a joke, it's not a joke anymore, it's a lecture. So in English I'm just... quiet. Nice. English me is very polite and very boring.
